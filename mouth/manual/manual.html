Words, without words.

Mouth is a synth engine that sings vowels and babbles made-up syllables in time with the OP-1. Turn a knob and the vowel changes; switch on talk and every key says its own word. Chords sound like a small choir.

About this manual

Mouth is a synth engine, so everything the OP-1 does around an engine still works as usual: the envelope, the effects, the LFOs, the octave, and saving sounds. Teenage Engineering's guide covers those: teenage.engineering/guides/op-1/original/synthesizer-mode. This manual covers Mouth's own knobs, screen and sounds.

How to read the controls

The OP-1 has four encoders. This manual calls them by colour, left to right: blue, green, white and orange.

  1. Turn an encoder to change a value. Push an encoder until it clicks to press it like a button.
  2. Press a key, or hold it while you do something else. "Shift + T1" means hold Shift and press T1.
  3. Text in a black box, such as talk 1/8, is what the screen shows.

Screens have numbered dots. The list beside the picture says what each number shows. Dot colour matches the encoder that changes that part.

Get started

  1. Install mouth.knl from the Hundekraft workshop's Your plugins page. Building it is described in the Mouth README.
  2. Press and pick a sound with a sound key, 1 to 8.
  3. Hold Shift and press T1. Turn blue to mouth and press T1.
  4. Hold a key. You hear a voice babbling around "ah": talk is on when Mouth is first picked. Talk explains how to switch it off.

To start from a finished sound instead, install the presets.

The screen

The mouth follows the first voice that is sounding. It is wide and smiling for "ee", open with the tongue showing for "ah", and puckered for "oh" and "oo". In talk, it presses shut for a "b" or "m", puckers for a "w" and smiles for a "y". Breath parts the lips, and vibrato makes it wobble. With no key held, it follows the knobs, so you can see a vowel before you play it.

A knob on its second page shows its page-2 name and a second line above its colour.

Sing

Page 1 shapes the voice. Every vowel and every body plays at about the same loudness, so you can sweep them without riding the volume.

KnobDoes
blue vowelThe vowel, gliding from ee through ih ay eh a ah aw oh to oo
green bodyWho is singing: giant, man, woman, child, tiny. It changes the size of the voice, not the note
white breathAir in the voice. Fully right is a whisper
orange vibratoLeft half: vibrato depth, up to a semitone each way. It fades in a quarter second after each note starts. Right half: the label reads warble and the vibrato speeds up to a fast warble
Tip

Mouth uses the OP-1's own envelope (T2). A slow attack and a long release turn it into a pad; a short decay with no sustain makes it a plucked "bah".

Chords: a small choir

Mouth plays six notes at once. Every voice drifts slightly in pitch and has its own vibrato, so a chord sounds like several singers rather than one voice played six times. With talk on, each one babbles separately.

Talk

Talk turns a held note into made-up syllables: the vowel hops around the one blue points at, and some syllables start with a quick closing, like "b", "m", "w" or "y". There are no real consonants, so it never says real words, but it sounds as if it does.

Talk lives on the second page of blue, green and white. Push a knob until it clicks to switch it to page 2, and push it again to switch back. Page 2 moves one step per click of the encoder.

Knob (page 2)Does
blue talkoff, or a syllable every 1/2 1/4 1/4t 1/8 1/8t 1/16 1/16t 1/32 note of the OP-1's tempo
green wordkey: each key says its own word. 1 to 16: every key says that word
white wild0 to 100%: how far the syllables wander and how sharp their edges are

When you let go of a key, talk stops making new syllables and the note fades out on its last vowel.

Words

A word is a fixed sequence of syllables. With word key, the key you press picks the word, so the same key always says the same thing and its neighbour says something else. Repeated, it starts to sound like a phrase, the way a chopped vocal does in a track.

Pick a number instead and every key says that one word at its own pitch, so a melody sings a single phrase.

Wild

  1. Low: the syllables stay close to the blue vowel and flow into each other: mumbling.
  2. Middle: chatty, with a mix of soft and sharp starts.
  3. High: the vowel jumps across the whole range, the starts are sharp, and now and then a syllable is held over the next beat, which gives the rhythm a spoken unevenness.

Even at 0% a little movement remains, so talk is always audible while it's on.

Tempo and the sequencer

Talk follows the OP-1's tempo (its BPM setting, or MIDI clock) and starts fresh with each note. Notes from a sequencer start on the beat, so their syllables land on it too. Change the tempo and the talk speeds up or slows down with it.

Talking with LFOs

The OP-1's LFOs can turn Mouth's knobs for you. Moving blue moves the vowel, and a moving vowel sounds like talking even with talk switched off. On a synth sound, T4 switches the LFO on and off, and Shift + T4 picks its type. Each type uses the four encoders differently (from TE's guide):

LFOEncodersWith Mouth
Valuespeed amount destination parameterDestination the synth, parameter blue: the vowel sweeps to and fro, a "wah-wah" when fast, a sigh when slow. On green the voice grows and shrinks instead. Most presets use this
Elementsource amount destination parameterSource the synth envelope, parameter blue: every note opens its vowel as it starts, like "wow". Source the g-force sensor: tilt the OP-1 to change the vowel. Source the mic: your own voice moves Mouth's
Randomamount speed destination attack/decayMoves vowel, body, breath and vibrato all at once. Keep the amount small for a restless voice
Tremolospeed pitch volume envelopePitch and volume only. Mouth's own vibrato usually does this better
Tip

Watch the blue mark on Mouth's screen: it shows where the LFO has pushed the vowel. The most word-like sound uses both: talk on for syllables, and a slow Value LFO on blue so the words drift.

Presets

Eight finished sounds come with Mouth, in the project's presets/mouth folder. Most of them have a Value LFO already on blue.

  1. Hold Shift and press COM, then press T3. The OP-1 shows up on the computer as a disk.
  2. Copy the mouth folder into the disk's synth folder, so the disk has synth/mouth/babble.aif and the rest.
  3. Eject the disk. The OP-1 restarts by itself, loads the sounds and waits for a key press.
  4. To load one, hold Shift and press a sound key. Turn blue to the mouth folder and green to a sound.
Warning

If the OP-1 shows Failed to create synth patch for each sound as it syncs, it is full. It holds a limited number of user synth sounds and deletes the ones it can't take. On Hundekraft that limit was reached with about 220 sounds on the disk, well under the 300 the Hundekraft preset collection mentions. Delete preset folders you don't use from the disk's synth folder, copy the mouth folder again, and eject.

Limits

  1. Six voices, as every OP-1 synth engine.
  2. Nine vowels and five bodies; no real consonants, so no real words.
  3. 16 fixed words, plus one per key.
  4. Talk follows tempos from 30 to 480 BPM.
  5. Velocity brightens the voice only when notes arrive with velocity, such as over MIDI: the original OP-1's keys always play at full.

Where it comes from

Mouth is a formant synth: a pulse like the vocal folds' passes through five resonances, the formants, that make one vowel sound different from another. The formant frequencies for each vowel and body come from CHIMERA, a physically modelled voice synthesizer by Jameson Hodge (MIT licence). CHIMERA simulates a whole airway; Mouth borrows its answers, which is how it fits on the OP-1.
